Your First Commission: How to Brief an Independent Artist Without Friction

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · October 28, 2026

"I want something... calm? But not boring. Like the feeling right before a storm, maybe, but warm?"

That's a real kind of message artists receive. It's not a bad message. It's an honest one. But it leaves the artist guessing, and guessing costs both of you time, money, and a little goodwill.

Most first-time buyers don't struggle with taste. They struggle with translation. You can feel exactly what you want and still have no words for it. The fix isn't becoming an art expert. It's handing the artist five pieces of information in a clear order.

There's one line in a good brief that almost nobody writes, and it saves more revisions than any other. We'll get there.

What is a commission brief, and why does it matter?

A commission brief is a short written summary of what you want, where it will live, and what you can spend. It matters because an artist can't read your mind, and a clear brief cuts revision rounds before they start.

Think of it as a handshake in writing. It protects your budget and respects the artist's time. It also forces you to figure out what you actually want, which is the real work.

What should a commission brief include?

A solid brief covers five things: feeling, space, dimensions, palette, and budget range. Write a sentence or two for each.

1. Feeling

Start here, because it's the part that makes the piece yours. Describe the emotion you want the room to hold. "Quiet courage." "A Sunday morning nobody interrupts." "Restless, but in a good way."

Then add the line almost nobody writes: what you do NOT want it to feel like. "Not sterile. Not cute. Not loud." A "not" list gives the artist guardrails faster than any compliment ever could.

2. Space

Say where it will hang and what's around it. A narrow hallway with no natural light behaves very differently from a living room wall with a big window. Include a photo of the wall if you can. Mention anything nearby, like a dark sofa or warm wood floors.

3. Dimensions

Give an actual size, in inches or centimeters. Tape a piece of paper or painter's tape to the wall at the size you're imagining and live with it for a day. Many first-timers go smaller than they should. If you're unsure, offer a range and ask the artist what they'd suggest.

4. Palette

You don't need color theory. Point to colors you love and colors that make you wince. A few reference images help, such as a photo of a sky, a fabric, or a painting by someone else. Reference images work best as mood, not as a request to copy another artist.

5. Budget range

Give a range, not a single number. "Between X and Y, framing separate" is clear and fair. Ranges let the artist tell you honestly what's possible, instead of quietly building something that doesn't fit or turning you down.

What happens from first message to delivery?

Most commissions follow the same arc, though every artist runs it a little differently. Here's a typical timeline you can expect.

Step 1: Inquiry. You send your brief. The artist replies with questions, availability, and whether your idea suits their style.

Step 2: Quote and agreement. You settle price, size, timeline, and what's included. Many artists ask for a deposit at this stage. Get the terms in writing, even if it's just a clear email thread.

Step 3: Sketch or concept. The artist shows a rough direction. This is your cheapest moment to change course. A different composition here costs minutes. The same change later can cost days.

Step 4: Revisions. Ask before you start how many rounds are included. One or two is common, though policies vary. Keep feedback specific: "the left side feels crowded" beats "I don't love it."

Step 5: Final work. The artist completes the piece and shows you images before it ships.

Step 6: Payment and delivery. You pay any balance, and the piece is packed and shipped or handed over.

Timelines swing widely based on the artist's queue and the medium, so ask for an honest estimate up front rather than assuming.

How do I give feedback without hurting the work?

Describe what you feel, not what you think the artist should do. "This reads tense to me, and I wanted calm" gives them a problem to solve. "Make the blue lighter" gives them a chore.

If you feel resistance to speaking up, that's common. Many first-time buyers worry that any critique is rude. A useful experiment: say one thing you love first, then one thing that's off. Notice whether the conversation gets easier. Artists generally want the piece to land, and clear notes help them get there.

If your gut says the draft is wrong in a way you can't explain, say that too. "Something's off and I can't name it yet" is a legitimate note. For more on trusting that instinct, see How to Tell If a Piece of Art Is Right for You.

What should I check before I send a deposit?

Confirm you're dealing with the actual artist, and that the terms are written down. Before paying, make sure you can answer these:

  • Does the artist have a portfolio that matches what you're asking for?
  • Are price, size, revision rounds, and timeline in writing?
  • Is the deposit amount and refund situation clear?
  • Do you know who owns what, such as whether you can display or reproduce the piece?

If any answer is fuzzy, ask. A good artist won't mind. For a deeper checklist, Questions Worth Asking an Artist Before You Buy Original Work Online walks through it.

FAQ

Q: How much should I budget for a first commission? There's no universal number, because price depends on the artist, medium, and size. Look at what an artist charges for finished work in their shop, then give a range that respects that. Remember to budget for framing and shipping separately.

Q: Do I need to know art terms to commission something? No. Plain words about feeling and space are more useful than jargon. "Warm, hushed, a little bit wild" tells an artist more than a borrowed art term you aren't sure you mean.

Q: What if I don't like the final piece? Raise it at the sketch stage, since that's where changes are easiest. Ask about the revision and refund policy before you pay, because custom work is often handled differently from shop items. Having terms in writing makes any hard conversation calmer.

Q: Can I ask an artist to copy another artist's style? It's better to use other work as mood reference and let the artist bring their own hand. Asking someone to imitate a living artist is awkward at best. Most artists will tell you what they can and can't take on.

Q: How long does a commission usually take? It varies with the artist's queue and the medium. Ask for an estimated window at the quote stage, and ask what could shift it.
