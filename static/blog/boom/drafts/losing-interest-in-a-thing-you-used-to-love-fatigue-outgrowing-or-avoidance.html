Losing Interest in a Thing You Used to Love: Fatigue, Outgrowing, or Avoidance?

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · November 13, 2026

Your guitar is on the stand, the camera is in the bag, the half-built project sits open on your laptop, and you feel nothing. Not hate. Just a flat, quiet "meh" where the spark used to be.

Then the second thought shows up, and it's the one that hurts: Was it ever real?

Hold that question for a minute. Because "is it normal" has a plain answer, and the answer is not "you're a fraud."

Is it normal to lose interest in what I used to love?

Yes, it's common, and it doesn't prove the passion was fake. Interest shifts for ordinary reasons, and most of them fall into three buckets: you're depleted, your values changed, or you're dodging the hard part of the craft.

The trouble is that all three feel identical from the inside. Flat. Bored. Vaguely guilty. So people spiral about authenticity when what they need is a diagnosis.

Here's a way to sort it out. For each cause, run one small, time-limited experiment. No big announcements, no quitting, no rebuilding your identity over a bad month.

Is it depletion? Test it with a rest-and-return experiment.

Depletion means the passion is intact but your reserves are empty. Loss of interest is a common companion of overload, poor recovery, and long stretches of pushing.

The signs: the disinterest isn't limited to this one thing. Food, friends, and other hobbies feel dull too. You're tired in a way sleep doesn't fully fix.

The experiment (7 days): Take the thing off your plate completely. No "light practice." Instead, add one low-demand block each day, like a walk without headphones. If you want ideas for building those gaps on purpose, Engineering Idle Time lays out how.

On day eight, open the craft for ten minutes. Notice whether curiosity shows up, even a small flicker. A flicker suggests depletion was a big part of the picture. Dead silence points you to the next two tests. Individual responses vary, and this is a check, not a verdict.

Is it changed values? Test it with a "what do I miss" experiment.

Changed values means the thing no longer matches what matters to you now. This isn't failure. People change, and some loves were built on goals you picked up from other people.

The signs: you feel relief, not guilt, when you imagine never doing it again. Or you still like the idea of it, but the actual daily work feels like it belongs to someone younger.

The experiment (10 days): Each evening, write one sentence finishing this: "What I actually miss about this is ___." Be specific. Maybe you miss the late-night flow, the feeling of being good at something, the way other people reacted, or the escape from a hard job.

Look at your ten sentences. If they point to something that still lives in you, such as making sound or building things with your hands, the craft may need a new shape, not an exit. If they all point to approval, status, or who you used to be, you may be outgrowing it. Finding Your Own Values After Years of Borrowed Goals is a good next read here.

Is it avoidance? Test it with a "smallest scary thing" experiment.

Avoidance means there's one specific hard part of the work you've been sidestepping, and the whole thing has started to feel dead because of it. Here's the information gap most people miss: the boredom often attaches to the whole craft when only one piece is the problem.

The signs: the disinterest got worse right after something got harder, like a finish line, a public release, a critique, or a skill plateau. You can still get excited about starting things, just not finishing or showing them.

The experiment (5 days): Name the single hardest part. Mixing, sharing, pricing, editing, the blank first page. Then do the smallest possible version of it for fifteen minutes a day. Not the full task, a sliver.

Watch what happens to your interest around that sliver. If the dread is loud but the work afterward feels alive, avoidance was likely driving the flatness. This pairs well with Frozen at the Blank Page, which separates fear from fatigue from an unclear next step.

What if more than one is true?

Then run them in order: depletion first, because a drained person can't read their own signals. Avoidance second, because it's cheap and fast. Changed values last, because it's the slowest and the most honest.

Write down your results after each test. Three short notes beat a month of vague feelings. If you want a structured way to pick a provisional direction while you sort this out, the 30 Day Direction Reset gives you a frame that doesn't require certainty.

Quick FAQ

Does losing interest mean I should quit? Not yet. Run the three tests first. Quitting can be right, but a diagnosis should come before the decision.

What if I feel nothing in all three tests? That's useful data too. Flatness across everything can point to something bigger than a craft problem, and talking with a doctor or therapist is a reasonable move.

How long should I give this? Roughly three weeks covers all three experiments. Set an end date so it doesn't become another way to drift.

Pick one test and start tonight

The spiral about authenticity feels productive, but it's just rumination in a nicer outfit. A test with an end date gives you real information.

Tonight, choose the cause that felt most true while you read, and put its first day on your calendar.
