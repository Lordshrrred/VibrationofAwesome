Attention as a Resource You Can Allocate on Purpose

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · November 5, 2026

Picture the last time you opened your phone to check one thing and surfaced forty minutes later holding a half-cold coffee and no memory of what you saw. Nobody made that choice. A feed made it for you, and then it sent you the bill in the form of a song you didn't finish.

Here is the claim that changes how this feels: attention is not something you lose. It is something you spend. The only open question is whether you set the budget or a product team did.

How do feeds designed to hold attention actually work?

Engagement-optimized feeds work by setting defaults that keep you moving and making stopping the effortful option. Three of them do most of the damage.

Autoplay removes the decision point. The next video starts before you've decided you want it, so continuing costs nothing and leaving costs a deliberate act.

Infinite scroll removes the natural stopping point. A page with an end asks "keep going?" A feed with no end never asks.

Notification badges create small open loops. A red number reads as unfinished business, and unfinished business pulls at a brain that likes closure.

None of this requires a villain. These features exist because they raise time-on-app, which is the number the product is measured by. Knowing that is useful, because defaults are the one thing you can actually change.

What does it mean to spend attention instead of lose it?

It means treating your attention like money: finite, trackable, and worth deciding about in advance. "I lost the afternoon" is a story where things happen to you. "I spent the afternoon" is a story where you can audit the purchase.

This reframe is practical, not poetic. A budget makes tradeoffs visible. If you spend ninety minutes on feeds, those ninety minutes are not available for the mix, the lyric, or the newsletter. You can still choose the feeds. You just choose them with the price tag showing.

For creators, the stakes are specific. Making things needs long, uninterrupted stretches. A day sliced into twelve-minute pieces by pings and refreshes is a day where deep work technically had time but never had room.

How do I run an attention allocation audit?

List the three things you most want attention for this month, then measure how much of your real day reaches them. That's the whole exercise, and it takes about twenty minutes.

Step 1: Name your three

Write the three things you most want to pour yourself into this month. Be concrete. "Finish the EP's final two tracks." "Write four newsletter issues." "Learn the new synth." Not "be more creative."

Step 2: Track one ordinary day

Use your phone's built-in screen time report (iOS Screen Time and Android Digital Wellbeing both show app-level totals). Add a paper tally of anything else that ate time. Do not fix anything yet. You are collecting data, not passing judgment.

Step 3: Sort the hours

Put each block of the day into one of four buckets: your three, necessary life, deliberate rest, and everything else. Be honest about the fourth one. Deliberate rest is a real category, and a movie you chose on purpose belongs there. A feed you opened on reflex does not.

Step 4: Read the gap

Compare the hours that reached your three against the hours that went to everything else. The number is information, not a verdict. Some people find they have more room than they thought. Others find the gap is the whole story.

There is one detail in the sorting most people skip, and it changes the results: tag each "everything else" block with what triggered it. Was it a badge, boredom, an autoplay queue, a stuck moment in the work? The trigger tells you which default to change first.

How do I take back the defaults?

Change the defaults, not your character. Willpower fights the feed at full strength all day. Settings fight it once.

  • Turn off autoplay in every app that offers the toggle.
  • Remove badge counts for anything that isn't a real person waiting on you. Keep the ones tied to people and income, drop the rest.
  • Move the apps off your home screen and into a folder on the second page, so opening one takes a few extra seconds. That small cost interrupts the reflex.
  • Batch your checking into set windows instead of leaving every feed permanently open in your pocket.

If you want a deeper look at how friction works, Friction Design: Making the Pull Cost You a Few Seconds walks through the idea. And The Input Budget: How Much Stimulation a Creative Day Can Actually Hold pairs well with this audit if your problem is more intake than interruption.

How does this apply when AI tools are in the mix?

AI tools can protect your attention or quietly spend it, depending on what you hand them. Delegating a repetitive task like reformatting show notes frees hours for your three. Delegating a thought you still need to have, like what the song is actually about, spends something you meant to keep.

A simple daily test: before you open a tool, ask whether it is saving you time on something mechanical or replacing a thought that is yours to think. The first is leverage. The second is a leak. FAQ

Q: Is it realistic to quit feeds completely as a musician who needs social media? No, and you don't need to. The goal is a budget, not abstinence. Decide how many minutes your promotion and research genuinely need, schedule them, and let the rest of the day belong to making things.

Q: Why can't I just use willpower to ignore the feed? Because feeds are built by teams whose job is to defeat willpower, and your willpower is a limited daily supply. Changing a default once, such as switching off autoplay, works without drawing on that supply again.

Q: How long should the allocation audit last? One ordinary day is enough to see a pattern, and a full week gives a steadier picture. Repeat it monthly when you pick your next three priorities, since your priorities and your leaks both shift.

Q: What if my "everything else" bucket is most of my day? Treat the number as data, not a failure. Start with the single biggest trigger you tagged, change just that one default this week, and re-measure. Small, specific changes hold better than a total overhaul.

Q: Does tracking my time risk making me more anxious about it? It can for some people, so keep the audit short and neutral. You're collecting information for a budget, not scoring yourself. If tracking feels stressful, limit it to one day and a single question: where did my three things lose out?

Spend It Where You Meant To

Pick your three things tonight, run the one-day audit tomorrow, and flip off autoplay before you go to bed.
