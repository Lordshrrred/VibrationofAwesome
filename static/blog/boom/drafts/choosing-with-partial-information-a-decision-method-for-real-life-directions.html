Choosing With Partial Information: A Decision Method for Real Life Directions

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · November 21, 2026

Blunt statement first: you will never feel sure enough, and the waiting is not neutral. Every month you spend circling two or three plausible directions is a month you picked a fourth option, which is drift.

You probably know the loop. You read another article, ask three friends, journal for an hour, and end up exactly where you started, except more tired. The problem is rarely a lack of information. It's that you are asking a feeling to do a job a method should do.

So here's a method. It uses criteria you set in advance, a scoring sheet, and a tiebreak rule that kicks in after seven days. It won't hand you certainty. It will hand you a choice.

How do I know which life direction to commit to?

You commit by scoring your options against criteria you wrote before you got emotional, then acting on the winner for a set trial period. You don't wait for certainty, because certainty usually shows up after commitment, not before it.

The Field Guide at VOA Field Guide goes deeper on the mindset side of this. The method below is the practical engine.

Start by naming two or three real options. Real means you could begin one this week. "Be happier" isn't an option. "Teach guitar lessons part time," "go all in on the album," and "stay in the current job and renegotiate hours" are options.

What criteria should I score each direction against?

Score each direction on three questions: what it requires you to build every day, what it costs you to abandon, and whether you would regret never trying it. These three cut through fantasy better than "which one excites me most."

Criterion 1: What would this require me to build daily?

Describe the Tuesday, not the highlight reel. If the direction is "full-time musician," Tuesday might be four hours of booking emails, two hours of practice, and an hour of editing video. If that Tuesday makes your stomach drop, that is data. If it makes you quietly curious, that is data too.

Criterion 2: What would it cost me to abandon it?

Some directions are cheap to drop after a month. Others burn money, relationships, or credibility on exit. This isn't a reason to avoid the expensive ones. It is a reason to know which kind you are testing, and to size your first step accordingly.

Criterion 3: Which one would I regret not trying?

Imagine yourself years from now, with each door closed. Which closed door bothers you? Regret of inaction is often louder than regret of a bad attempt. Pay attention to which option you keep returning to when nobody is watching.

If you already have two options that both fit, this tiebreaker approach pairs well with the sheet below.

How do I build the scoring sheet?

Draw a table with your options as columns and the three criteria as rows, then score each cell from 1 to 5 before you discuss it with anyone. Do it in one sitting so your mood doesn't rewrite the numbers halfway through.

Here is the layout:

Option A Option B Option C
Daily build: do I want that Tuesday? (1-5)
Cost to abandon: how cheap is the exit? (1-5, 5 = cheap)
Regret if never tried (1-5)
Total

Two rules keep the sheet honest. Write the criteria weights first if some matter more to you (maybe regret counts double). And write a one-line reason for every score, because a number with no reason is just a mood in disguise.

The sheet surfaces something useful even when the totals are close: you see which criterion is driving your pull. That tells you what you actually value, which is rarely what you assumed.

What if the scores come out tied or feel wrong?

If scores tie or the winner feels wrong, run a seven-day trial of the top two and let the lived experience break the tie. A gut reaction to a number on paper is information, but a week of actual behavior is better information.

Here is the tiebreak rule. For seven days, spend a small, fixed block on each tied option, say 45 minutes a day. Do the real work, not research about the work. On day seven, answer one question for each: did I look forward to the next session, or did I negotiate my way out of it?

Resist the urge to read this as a verdict on your worth. A direction that loses the tiebreak isn't wrong forever. Direction can change over time, and that is allowed. You are choosing a current heading, not signing a lifetime contract.

If the "wrong feeling" is strong, check what it's made of. Fear of being visible can feel like "this isn't right." So can ordinary grief at closing a door. Grieving the path you are leaving is worth reading if that sounds familiar.

What happens after I pick one?

After you pick, commit for a fixed window, thirty days works well, and review once a week with one question: what did this week teach me about whether to continue? The review keeps commitment from turning into stubbornness.

There is one thing almost nobody does here, and it changes the whole experience: they write down, before starting, what would count as a reason to stop. A pre-set exit condition (money spent, hours logged, a specific discomfort that persists) lets you commit fully, because the door isn't welded shut. Without it, every hard day becomes a referendum on your entire life.

Purpose in this frame isn't discovered like buried treasure. It's built through choices, constraints, and feedback. The commitment creates the information. That's the quiet trick behind committing before you feel ready.

FAQ

Q: How long should I test a direction before deciding? Thirty days is a practical default for a first test, with a seven-day tiebreak when two options tie. That is long enough to hit the boring middle of the work and short enough that quitting stays cheap. Longer, riskier moves deserve a longer trial and a smaller first step.

Q: What if I score everything low? A uniformly low sheet usually means your options are too vague or none of them is the real option. Rewrite them as concrete Tuesdays, or ask what direction you keep avoiding writing down. Low scores can also point to exhaustion, in which case rest comes before choosing.

Q: Can I pick two directions at once? You can, if one is the main trial and the other is a small side experiment with a strict time cap. Splitting evenly usually produces two half-tests and no real data. Give one direction most of your effort and keep the second small.

Q: What if I commit and later realize I chose wrong? Then you have better information than you started with, which is the point of testing. Review against the exit conditions you wrote, adjust, and pick again. A direction that didn't fit still narrows your criteria for the next sheet.

Q: Is it okay to change direction after a few years? Yes. People and circumstances change, and a heading that fit at thirty may not fit at forty. Changing direction after honest effort is different from drifting. The test is whether you chose it, not whether you stayed forever.

Make the choice this week

Grab a sheet of paper, write your two or three real options, and fill in the scoring table in one sitting today. Then pick your thirty-day test and write your exit conditions on the same page.
