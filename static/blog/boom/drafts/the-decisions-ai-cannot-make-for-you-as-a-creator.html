The Decisions AI Cannot Make For You as a Creator

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · October 22, 2026

A chorus you can't explain. A caption that sounds like a stranger wearing your name. A draft that is clean, finished, and somehow not yours.

If you use AI for creative work, this moment finds you eventually. The tool gave you something good enough, and "good enough" is exactly the problem.

The fix is not using AI less. It is deciding in advance what the tool never gets to decide.

What decisions should I keep when using AI for creative work?

Keep four: the premise, which draft feels true, what gets cut, and what the work is for. Everything else is delegable. These four are where your judgment lives.

The premise

The premise is the reason the piece exists. "A song about leaving a town you outgrew" is a premise. "Write me a song about change" is a prompt. When the tool picks the premise, you are editing someone else's idea.

Which draft feels true

AI will hand you ten versions in a minute. Only you know which one matches what actually happened or what you actually believe. That test happens in your body, not in the output.

What to cut

Models tend to add. Editors subtract. Cutting is where taste shows up, and it is the move most people skip when a draft arrives already polished.

What the work is for

Is this track for the people who found you through your last record, or is it a bid for a playlist? A tool can't know your audience the way you do, and it can't want anything on your behalf.

How do I audit an AI-assisted draft for my own voice?

Read the draft line by line and mark every line you could not defend out loud in your own voice. A defendable line is one where you can say why it is there, in your own words, without checking the chat window.

Step 1: Print it or paste it somewhere neutral

Move the draft out of the AI interface. A plain document gives you distance, and it stops the tool's formatting from lending everything false authority.

Step 2: Mark the lines you could not defend

Highlight any line where you would shrug if someone asked, "Why this word? Why this image?" Do not fix anything yet. Just mark.

Step 3: Sort the marks

Each marked line gets one of three tags: rewrite (the idea is right, the wording isn't), replace (wrong idea), or cut. Resist the urge to keep a line because it sounds impressive. Sounding impressive is the machine's whole talent.

Step 4: Read the survivors aloud

Whatever is left should sound like something you would say on a bad Tuesday and a good one. If a sentence needs a performance voice to survive, it is probably not yours.

This also pairs well with recovering your actual creative signal after extended imitation, which covers what to do when the marks outnumber the keepers.

Why does AI-assisted work start to feel generic?

AI output drifts toward the middle of what it has seen, so unchecked drafts sound like everyone. Your specifics, odd word choices, and strange angles are what pull a piece away from that middle.

Notice the pattern in tools you already use. AI music tools can make everything sound the same when the human accepts defaults. The defaults are not evil. They are just average, and average is not a voice.

There is one move almost nobody makes here, and it is cheap: before you generate anything, write down two sentences about what you want the piece to feel like and one thing it must not sound like. Then judge every output against those sentences, not against the output before it. Without that anchor, each draft only competes with the last one, and your standards slide without you noticing.

How do I build a retained-decisions list before I publish?

Write a short list of decisions you personally made on this piece, and refuse to publish until every item is true. Five lines is plenty.

Try this version as an experiment:

  • I chose the premise, and I can state it in one sentence.
  • I picked this draft because it felt true, not because it was first or longest.
  • I cut at least one thing the tool wanted to keep.
  • I know what this work is for and who it is for.
  • Every line passes the "could I defend this" audit.

If one box stays empty, that is your next edit. It is a checklist from ordinary editorial practice, not a claim about how machines think. Editors have always asked who decided what, and creators using AI can borrow the habit.

FAQ

Q: Is it still my work if AI helped draft it? It can be, as long as you made the decisions that define the piece. Authorship in practice comes down to who chose the premise, shaped the final selection, and stands behind every line. If you can defend each choice, the work carries your judgment.

Q: Should I tell people I used AI? Disclosure depends on your platform, your audience, and the rules of whoever publishes or distributes your work. Check the terms where you release, and lean toward being plain about your process if your audience would care.

Q: How much of a draft should I rewrite? There is no fixed percentage. Rewrite whatever fails the audit, and judge by voice, not by word count. A draft where you changed 20 percent but every key choice is yours beats one where you rewrote everything but never chose the premise.

Q: What if I can't tell which lines feel like me? Read the draft aloud, or compare it to something you wrote before using AI. Lines that you stumble over or would never say in conversation are usually the ones to mark first.

Q: Does using AI for ideas weaken my taste? It can if you accept outputs without judging them, since taste grows from choosing and rejecting. Use the tool to widen the options, then practice rejecting most of them on purpose.
