Finding Your Own Values After Years of Borrowed Goals

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · November 3, 2026

You got the degree, the job, the apartment, maybe the relationship, and every box was checked by someone else's pencil. Now someone asks what you want, and you hear nothing. Not a wrong answer. Nothing.

That silence is not proof you have no values. It is what happens when nobody ever asked you to use them. Borrowed goals come with borrowed reasons, and after enough years the reasons sound like your own voice.

Here is the part most advice skips: you do not find your values by thinking harder about them. You find them by looking at evidence you already generated without noticing.

How do I figure out what I value when I've always followed someone else's plan?

Start with your own behavior, not with a list of virtue words. Values show up in what you chose when nobody was grading you, so your history is better data than a quiz.

Abstract lists like "freedom, creativity, integrity" feel nice and predict nothing. They are the motivational poster version. Skip them for now. You are going to collect three lists of specific, small, true things instead.

If you want a wider map of how this fits into rebuilding a life, the Field Guide lays out the larger picture. The exercise below works on its own.

Step 1: List the times you lost track of time

Write down 8 to 12 activities where hours disappeared. Be concrete. Not "being creative" but "rearranging the garage shelves for three hours on a Saturday" or "helping a friend untangle a messy problem."

Include things that seem silly or unproductive. Include things nobody paid you for. The point is absorption, because absorption tends to happen around things that matter to you.

Look for patterns in what the activities share. Was it building something, solving something, being around people, or being alone with a hard problem?

Step 2: List the choices you felt proud of that you made alone

Write down 5 to 8 moments where you made a choice without anyone's approval and felt quietly good about it afterward. Maybe you left a conversation that was going nowhere. Maybe you said no to a favor, or spent money on something odd but right.

The detail that matters here: pride in a choice usually points at a value you honored, even when the choice was small or costly. Write what you did, then one sentence on why it felt good.

Step 3: List the things you do only because you were told to

Now flip it. Write 8 to 12 things you do mainly out of obligation, habit, or fear of what someone would say. Be honest and specific. "Answering every family group text within an hour." "Dressing for a job I dread."

Sit with the list for a minute. Some of these you may keep on purpose, and that is fine. The goal is not to burn everything down. The goal is to see which ones you chose and which ones you inherited.

How do I turn those three lists into actual values?

Circle the repeating themes across all three lists, then write two or three plain-language statements of what those themes protect. A working value is a sentence you can use to make a decision, not a word you can put on a wall.

Here is a sample translation:

  • Lost-time list: building things with my hands, teaching someone, long walks with no phone
  • Proud-choice list: left a draining group, charged fair money for my work
  • Told-to list: constant availability, performing cheerfulness at work

Possible working values: "I protect uninterrupted time to build things." "I would rather be useful than liked." "I do not trade my energy for approval."

Notice these are specific enough to argue with. That is the point. A vague value cannot be wrong, which means it cannot help you.

How do I test whether these are really my values?

Run each value through ordinary decisions for two weeks and watch what happens. A value you merely admire will feel like homework. A value that is yours will make some decisions faster and a few uncomfortably clear.

Try this small experiment. Before a decision, even a minor one like whether to accept an invitation, ask: "Which of my three values does this serve or cost?" Write the answer in one line. At the end of two weeks, read the lines back.

You are looking for two signals. Which value kept showing up as the tiebreaker? And which one did you quietly ignore every time? The ignored one may be a borrowed value wearing a costume, or it may be a real value you are afraid of. Both are worth knowing.

Expect resistance. Following a plan is easier than reading your own signals, and some of your new values may disappoint people who liked the old you. That friction does not mean the value is wrong. It means it is yours.

FAQ

Q: What if my lists are mostly empty? A short list is still usable, so work with what you have. Many people who lived by other people's expectations have little record of choosing, which is exactly why small experiments matter. Try new low-stakes activities for a few weeks and add them to the lists.

Q: Can a value I was taught still be a value I choose? Yes, a taught value becomes yours when you keep it after examining it. The question is not where it came from but whether you would pick it again knowing what it costs.

Q: How many values should I end up with? Two or three is enough to start. More than that and you cannot use them in a quick decision. You can refine them over time as the experiments teach you something.

Q: What if my values conflict with my current life? Treat the conflict as information, not an emergency. Change one small thing that moves your daily life toward the value before making any large move, and see how it feels over a month.

Pick one value and make it cost you something small this week

Do the three lists tonight, write your two or three working values, and use one in a real decision before the weekend. Small, specific evidence builds self-trust faster than any big declaration.
