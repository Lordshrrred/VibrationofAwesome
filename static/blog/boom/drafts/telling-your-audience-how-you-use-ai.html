Telling Your Audience How You Use AI

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · November 7, 2026

Most creators who go quiet about AI aren't lying. They're dodging a conversation they don't know how to start. The cost shows up later, when a fan finds out sideways and wonders what else got left out.

Here's the part nobody mentions: vague wording hurts you more than the AI use itself. "Made with a little help from technology" sounds like a magician hiding a card. A plain sentence about what a tool actually did sounds like a craftsperson. The difference is accuracy, and accuracy can be learned.

How do I explain AI use without losing my audience's trust?

Say exactly what the tool did, what you did, and what the audience can expect from you going forward. Trust drops when people feel a gap between what they assumed and what happened, so your job is to close that gap before they find it.

Three parts, in this order:

  1. The tool's role. One concrete verb. "Generated a rough chord progression." "Suggested mastering settings." "Cleaned up stems."
  2. Your role. What you wrote, rebuilt, performed, chose, or rejected.
  3. The expectation. What this means for future work. "This is how I'll handle drafts going forward" or "this was a one-off experiment."

Skip the apology. Skip the lecture. A disclosure is a label, not a confession.

What should I actually say about what the AI did?

Name the specific task and the specific tool category, not a vague gesture at "AI assistance." Specific language reads as confident. Vague language reads as hiding.

Compare these two:

  • "This track was made with AI."
  • "I used an AI generator for a rough harmonic sketch, then rebuilt the chords, melody, and arrangement by hand."

The second one tells a listener what to hear. It also happens to be the more honest picture of your process. If your use was smaller, say smaller. If it was bigger, say bigger. The practical trust cost of overclaiming or underclaiming is the same: someone eventually checks.

How do I describe my own part without sounding defensive?

Describe your decisions, not your feelings about the decisions. Defensive writing explains why you're allowed. Confident writing just reports what happened.

Your part usually falls into one of these buckets: writing, rewriting, performing, selecting, rejecting, and mixing. Selecting and rejecting count. If you ran twenty generations and kept one, that curation is real authorship work, and you can say so plainly: "I generated options and chose the one that fit, then rewrote the bridge."

A useful test: read your disclosure out loud. If it sounds like you're asking permission, cut the first clause. If it sounds like you're picking a fight, cut the adjectives.

What does this look like for a real track?

Take one track through a generate-then-rebuild pass and mark each element honestly. A simple checklist keeps you accurate:

  • AI-touched: which elements started from a tool (drum pattern, chord sketch, lyric draft, cover art concept)
  • Rewritten by hand: which of those you rebuilt, replayed, or rewrote
  • Untouched by AI: which elements came entirely from you
  • To verify before release: the terms of the tool you used, your distributor's current AI policy, and any rules about the platforms you're uploading to

That last line is a prompt to check, not settled advice. Tool terms and platform rules change, and some claims you'll read online about legal risk are not sourced. Read the actual terms for the tool you used. This kind of note-keeping is exactly what the AI Engine guide is built to support, since a workflow you can describe is a workflow you can disclose.

What wording works in different situations?

Match the length to the venue, but keep the three parts intact.

Release notes or liner text: "Drums and the opening chord sketch started in an AI tool. I rewrote the harmony, played the lead, and mixed it myself."

Social caption (short): "Used AI for the first rough draft of the beat. Everything you hear on top is me."

Newsletter (the longer version): "Here's how I worked on this one. A generator gave me a rough arrangement idea, I threw out most of it, and I kept a rhythm pattern I then replayed. From here on, I'll note it whenever a tool did more than cleanup."

When someone asks you directly: "Yes, I used it for [task]. The [parts] are mine. Happy to walk through it."

If you want to check whether your process is quietly drifting toward the tool's defaults, reading your own output for drift after AI use is a good pass to run before you write any of this.

When should I disclose, and where should it live?

Disclose where your audience will actually see it, at the moment it's relevant. A buried "About" page paragraph protects nobody. Put it in the release notes, the caption, or the video description of the specific work.

Many creators find it easier to keep one standing page that describes their general process, then link to it. That way individual posts can stay short. For the larger question of which decisions stay yours no matter what the tool offers, the decisions AI cannot make for you as a creator is worth a read.

FAQ

Q: Do I have to disclose AI use on every single piece of work? There's no universal rule, so the practical answer is to disclose wherever leaving it out could mislead someone. If AI shaped the sound, the lyrics, or the art in a way a listener would care about, say it. For minor cleanup like noise reduction, a standing process page may be enough. Check any platform or distributor policy that applies to you.

Q: Will saying I used AI make people think my work is less real? It can for some people, and for others it makes no difference. What reliably damages trust is being found out after saying nothing. A specific, calm description of your role gives people something concrete to judge, which is better than leaving them to imagine the worst.

Q: Should I name the specific AI tool I used? Naming the tool is usually fine and often helpful, since it shows you aren't hiding anything. Name the category at minimum, such as "an AI music generator" or "an AI mastering assistant." If the tool's terms affect how you can release the work, that's a separate thing to verify on your own.

Q: What if I used AI early and then rewrote almost everything? Say that, because it's accurate. "AI gave me a starting sketch that I mostly replaced" is a true and useful sentence. Describe the starting point and the replacement honestly, and let listeners decide what it means to them.

Write Your Disclosure Before You Need It

Draft your three-part statement once, now, while nobody is asking. Then keep a running note of which tool did what on each project so the sentence is easy to write later.
