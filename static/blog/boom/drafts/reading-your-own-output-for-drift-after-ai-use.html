Reading Your Own Output for Drift After AI Use

Vibration of Awesome · AI-assisted · November 1, 2026

Here is the question hiding under your unease: if a stranger read ten of your old pieces and ten of your new ones, could they tell the same person wrote both? You can't answer that by feel. You're too close to the work, and your ear gets trained by whatever you hear most.

So stop asking your gut. Run a test on the pages.

How do I know if AI is changing my creative voice?

You know by comparing a sample of work from before you used AI against a sample from after, then marking the habits that appear in the new pile and not the old one. Feeling "off" is a signal to investigate. It isn't proof of anything.

The method below takes about an hour. It works for lyrics, captions, newsletters, bios, and song descriptions. Musicians can add arrangement notes and track structure.

Step 1: Pull ten old pieces and ten recent ones

Gather ten pieces from before you adopted AI tools and ten from the last few months. Pick work you actually finished and published, not drafts you abandoned.

Put them in two folders or two documents. Label them only "A" and "B" if you can, and shuffle the order so you can't lean on dates. The point is to read the work, not your memory of making it.

If you don't have ten old pieces, use what you have. Even five against five shows patterns.

Step 2: Mark repeated phrasing you didn't choose

Read each pile and highlight any phrase, opener, or closer that shows up three or more times. Then ask one blunt question about each highlight: did I pick this, or did it arrive?

Common AI-flavored repeats include:

  • Openers that announce a feeling ("There's something about...")
  • Tidy three-item lists
  • Closers that wrap everything with a lesson
  • Words like "journey," "tapestry," "resonate," and "unlock"

Count the highlights per pile. A jump from near zero in the old work to a dozen in the new work is real data.

Step 3: Check structure and tone, not just words

Phrasing is the easy part to spot. Structure drifts quietly. For each piece, note three things:

  • Shape: Does every piece follow the same arc, like setup, twist, neat resolution?
  • Temperature: Is the tone evenly warm and agreeable, where your old work had edges, jokes, or bluntness?
  • Specifics: Count the concrete details, like a named street, an odd instrument, an actual number. Generic drift usually shows up as fewer specifics.

Musicians can run the same check on tracks. Look at song length, section order, chord movement, and how your hooks land. If every recent song has the same intro-verse-chorus skeleton that a tool suggested, that's structure you inherited, not structure you designed.

If you want a closer look at why drafts go flat, The Lifeless Draft Problem breaks down the diagnosis.

Step 4: Sort every habit into keep, revise, or stop

Now make decisions. Take each repeated habit and put it in one of three columns:

  • Keep: You'd choose this on purpose. It sounds like you, or it taught you something good.
  • Revise: The instinct is fine but the wording is stock. Rewrite it in your own words.
  • Stop: You didn't choose it and you don't like it. Ban it from your prompts and your edits.

Not all change is erasure. Some of it is growth, and some of it is you borrowing a sentence rhythm that genuinely improved your work. Evolution vs. Erasure is useful for telling the two apart.

The one move most people skip

Almost nobody checks the old pile for habits they'd like to bring back. Scan your pre-AI work for things the new work lacks: a strange metaphor, a rude joke, a long messy sentence. Those missing pieces often name your voice better than the repeated phrases do.

Step 5: Turn the audit into a repeatable habit

Run this audit again in a month, using fresh pieces. Keep your "stop" list somewhere visible and paste it into your prompts as constraints. For a workflow that protects your sound as you go, Keeping Your Sound When Software Starts Making Suggestions pairs well with this.

For music specifically, a "generate, then rebuild" pass helps. For one track, list which elements an AI tool touched, which you rewrote by hand, and which you left alone. If you plan to release it, treat the legal side as something to check, not assume. Read the terms of any tool you used and look up current guidance on AI-assisted work before you publish.

FAQ

Q: Can AI change my voice without me noticing? Yes, it can, because repeated exposure to suggestions shapes what feels natural to you. The audit above exists for exactly this reason, since drift tends to show up in patterns across many pieces, not in any single one.

Q: How many pieces do I need to compare? Ten against ten is a solid sample, but five against five still reveals repeated habits. More pieces give clearer patterns, and fewer pieces give you a quick first read.

Q: What if I never published anything before using AI? Use any writing or recordings from before you adopted the tools, like old journals, voice memos, emails, or demos. Your unpolished work still carries your rhythm and word choices.

Q: Does using AI always make my work generic? No. Generic results usually come from accepting default output without editing it or giving the tool specific constraints. Using AI for ideation or cleanup, then rebuilding by hand, tends to preserve more of your own signal.

Q: How often should I repeat the audit? Once a month works for most creators, or after any major change in your tools or workflow. Short, regular checks catch drift before it becomes your default sound.
